After a Final Rejection: What Your Options Actually Are

A final rejection from the USPTO is not the end of your application and this guide explains the real choices you have and what each one costs you

2026-11-05 · patent prosecution · office action · final rejection · rce

A final rejection sounds definitive, but it rarely is. The USPTO issues final rejections routinely, and applicants have several well-established paths forward — each with different costs, timelines, and strategic consequences. Understanding those options before you respond matters, because the choice you make shapes not just this application but the patent rights you can claim for years.

What "Final" Actually Means

A final rejection closes the normal back-and-forth examination cycle. After a first Office Action, you file a response. If the examiner is not persuaded, the next rejection is usually designated final. That label does not mean the USPTO has permanently rejected your application — it means you have moved out of the standard response window and into a different set of procedural options.

You still have time to act. The standard response period after a final rejection is three months, extendable to six months with fees. Missing that window abandons the application, so the clock matters.

The Four Main Paths Forward

File a Response Under Rule 116

You can still file an amendment after a final rejection. The difference is that the examiner has discretion to enter it or not. Amendments that place the application in condition for allowance — meaning they resolve the rejection without raising new issues — are more likely to be entered. Amendments that introduce new claim language the examiner would need to re-examine from scratch are often refused.

This path works best when you have a narrow fix: canceling a rejected claim, amending to language the examiner suggested, or adding an argument you overlooked before. It is not a reliable vehicle for a full re-do.

File a Request for Continued Examination

An RCE is the most common response to a final rejection. It reopens prosecution — meaning the examiner picks up where they left off and issues a new, non-final Office Action. You pay a government fee, and examination continues.

An RCE is appropriate when you need more room to negotiate: when you want to submit additional prior art, restructure claims significantly, or present arguments the examiner has not fully considered. The tradeoff is cost and time. Each RCE adds months and fees, and some applications cycle through multiple RCEs before allowance or abandonment.

File a Notice of Appeal

You can appeal the examiner's rejection to the Patent Trial and Appeal Board. The PTAB reviews the examiner's legal conclusions — whether the rejections are well-supported under the applicable statutes — not whether your invention is commercially valuable or whether a different claim set might work.

Appeals take time, often well over a year. They also require a written brief explaining why the examiner was wrong, which is a different kind of work than drafting a response. If you win, the application returns to examination. If you lose, you can appeal further to federal court, but most applicants do not.

Appeals make strategic sense when the examiner's rejection has a clear legal flaw — an anticipation rejection based on a reference that does not actually disclose your claimed element, for example — and when you are unwilling to narrow your claims further.

File a Continuation Application

You can file a continuation before the application abandons, preserving your priority date while starting a new prosecution track with a new set of claims. This does not resolve the rejected application — that application may eventually go abandoned — but it gives you a fresh examination environment.

Some applicants file a continuation and an RCE simultaneously to keep multiple options open. That approach has costs, but it avoids being locked into one path before you see how the examiner responds.

What Happens If You Do Nothing

If you let the response deadline pass without filing, the application goes abandoned. An abandoned application can sometimes be revived if the delay was unintentional, but revival is not guaranteed, adds cost, and creates gaps in your prosecution history that can complicate later proceedings. Abandonment should be a deliberate choice, not a default.

Practical Takeaways

  • A final rejection is a procedural checkpoint, not a closed door — you have multiple routes forward.
  • Rule 116 amendments after final are discretionary; save them for narrow, clean fixes the examiner is likely to accept.
  • An RCE reopens prosecution fully and is the right tool when you need meaningful room to negotiate claims or arguments.
  • Appeals are appropriate when the examiner's rejection has a legal flaw, not simply when you disagree with the outcome.
  • Continuation applications let you pursue different claim scope without being bound by the current prosecution's constraints.
  • Watch the deadline carefully — abandonment by inaction is almost never the right strategic choice, and revival is uncertain.
