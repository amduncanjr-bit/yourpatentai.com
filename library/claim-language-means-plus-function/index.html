Means-Plus-Function Claim Language: What It Covers and Why It Narrows More Than You Think

Means-plus-function claim language gives software and hardware inventors a shorthand for describing what a component does, but the trade-off is a scope that is often far narrower than it appears on the page

2026-11-18 · means-plus-function · claim drafting · software patents · patent prosecution

When you draft a patent claim, you have choices about how to describe each element. One option is to describe what a component does rather than what it is — for example, "means for processing the input" instead of "a processor configured to execute the following steps." That phrasing triggers a specific legal rule under 35 U.S.C. § 112(f), and the consequences for your patent's scope are significant enough that every inventor and founder working on software or hardware should understand them before a claim gets filed.

What Means-Plus-Function Language Actually Is

Section 112(f) of the patent statute allows a claim element to be expressed as a "means" for performing a function, without reciting the structure that performs it. The tradeoff is explicit in the statute: the claim is construed to cover only the corresponding structure, material, or acts described in the specification — and equivalents thereof.

In plain terms: the word "means" in a claim element is a signal that the scope of that element is not defined by the words in the claim. It is defined by whatever structure you described in your written specification as performing that function.

  • If your specification describes one algorithm for processing user input, your "means for processing" covers that algorithm and structures equivalent to it — not every possible way to accomplish the same result.
  • If your specification describes no corresponding structure at all, the claim element may be found indefinite, which can invalidate the claim entirely.

The Software Problem

For software inventors, this rule creates a specific trap. Courts have held that for a software-implemented function, the corresponding structure is the algorithm — the specific steps the software executes — not just "a computer" or "a processor." A claim that recites "means for generating a recommendation" and a specification that says only "the system generates a recommendation using known techniques" describes no algorithm at all. That claim is likely indefinite.

This matters during prosecution because an examiner can reject a means-plus-function claim for indefiniteness under § 112 if the specification does not disclose adequate corresponding structure. It also matters after the patent issues, because a competitor challenging your patent can make the same argument in litigation or in an inter partes review proceeding.

The practical rule: if you use means-plus-function language, your specification must describe the specific algorithm, process, or structure that performs the recited function — in enough detail that someone could identify what you are actually claiming.

When It Applies Even Without the Word "Means"

Courts and the USPTO have extended § 112(f) beyond claims that use the literal word "means." Nonce words — generic functional nouns like "module," "unit," "element," "mechanism," and "device" — can trigger means-plus-function treatment if the claim provides no structural context for the term.

"A processing module for authenticating users" may be construed as a means-plus-function element depending on how the claim and specification are written. If the specification does not explain what that module actually does in structural or algorithmic terms, you face the same indefiniteness risk.

This has become a live issue in software patent prosecution. Examiners increasingly scrutinize claims that use modular language without structural support. If you receive an indefiniteness rejection that cites § 112(f), the examiner is telling you that your claim language reads as a function wrapper with nothing underneath it.

How to Respond to a § 112(f) Rejection

  • Amend the claim to add structural language that takes the element out of means-plus-function treatment — describe the structure or the algorithm directly in the claim.
  • Amend the specification to add corresponding structure if the claim language will stay functional — but be careful, because adding new matter after filing is not permitted; you can only clarify what is already there.
  • Argue that § 112(f) does not apply by explaining that the claim term has sufficient structural meaning in the relevant art and that the specification supports that reading.

Practical Takeaways

  • Means-plus-function claim elements are construed narrowly — only to the structure described in your specification and its equivalents, not to the full scope of the function.
  • For software, the corresponding structure must be an algorithm, not just a reference to a computer or processor.
  • Generic modular terms like "module," "unit," and "mechanism" can trigger means-plus-function treatment even without the word "means."
  • A means-plus-function element with no corresponding structure in the specification is likely indefinite and vulnerable to rejection or invalidation.
  • When responding to a § 112(f) rejection, your strongest option is usually amending the claim to include explicit structural or algorithmic language rather than relying on specification arguments alone.
  • Review your specification before filing to confirm that every functionally described element has a concrete, identifiable structure or algorithm supporting it.
