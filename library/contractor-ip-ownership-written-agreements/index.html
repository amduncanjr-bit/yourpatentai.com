Contractor IP Ownership: Why Who You Hire Determines Who Owns What You Build

When you hire an independent contractor to build something patentable, the default ownership rules are almost certainly not what you expect, and a missing or poorly drafted agreement can cost you rights you assumed you had.

2026-11-28 · contractor · ip ownership · assignment · employment

Most founders and product teams assume that paying someone to build something means the company owns it. That assumption is usually correct for employees — but it is frequently wrong for independent contractors, and the consequences can surface at the worst possible moment: a funding round, an acquisition, or a patent filing.

The Default Rule Nobody Remembers

Under U.S. patent law, the inventor owns the invention. Full stop. Payment for services does not change that. When an independent contractor conceives of a patentable invention — a novel algorithm, a new system architecture, an original method — that contractor is the inventor, and the contractor owns the rights unless there is a written agreement that says otherwise.

This is different from copyright, where the "work made for hire" doctrine can assign ownership to a hiring party under specific conditions. Patent law has no equivalent doctrine. There is no automatic transfer based on the nature of the engagement or the fact that you paid for the work. If you want to own what a contractor invents for you, you need a written assignment.

What a Valid Assignment Actually Requires

An assignment of patent rights must be in writing to be recorded at the USPTO and to be enforceable against third parties. Courts have generally required that an assignment be signed by the assignor — the contractor — to be effective. A statement in a contract that the company "shall own" any inventions is a promise to assign, which is better than nothing but weaker than an actual present-tense assignment. The difference matters in litigation and in due diligence.

Language to Watch For

  • "Hereby assigns" — present-tense language that transfers rights at the moment of signing. This is the stronger form.
  • "Agrees to assign" — a promise of future transfer. This creates a contractual obligation but does not automatically vest ownership in the company without a follow-up act.
  • Scope clauses — the assignment should cover U.S. and foreign patent rights, continuations, divisionals, and reissues, not just the initial application.
  • Moral rights and other non-U.S. concepts — if your contractor is based outside the U.S., local law may require additional steps or impose limits on what can be assigned.

The Timing Problem

Assignment agreements signed after the work is done are harder to enforce than those signed before work begins. A contractor who has already delivered something patentable has leverage you did not have at the start of the engagement. Get the agreement signed before any inventive work starts.

Common Gaps That Create Real Problems

Using a generic contract template. Many standard freelance or consulting agreements include IP provisions that were drafted for copyright, not patents. They may contain work-for-hire language that is legally effective for the code but does nothing for the underlying patentable methods.

Forgetting prior inventions carve-outs. A contractor may come to your project with pre-existing technology they intend to keep. If the assignment agreement is drafted too broadly, you may inadvertently claim ownership of something they invented before your engagement — which creates a dispute, not protection. A well-drafted agreement identifies prior inventions explicitly.

Multiple contractors, one invention. If two or more contractors jointly conceive of an invention, each is a joint inventor under U.S. law, and each joint owner can license the patent to anyone without the other's consent and without sharing proceeds. If your project involves multiple contributors, the assignment agreement needs to cover all of them.

Subcontractors you did not hire directly. If your primary contractor brings in a subcontractor who contributes to the invention, your agreement with the primary contractor does not automatically bind the subcontractor. You need a direct written assignment from every inventor.

Practical Takeaways

  • Patent rights do not transfer automatically when you pay a contractor — you need a written assignment signed by the contractor before inventive work begins.
  • "Agrees to assign" is weaker than "hereby assigns"; use present-tense assignment language when drafting or reviewing contractor agreements.
  • Review IP provisions in contractor templates carefully — work-for-hire clauses address copyright, not patent ownership.
  • Identify prior inventions explicitly in the agreement so the contractor retains what they brought in and you clearly own what was created for you.
  • If multiple contractors contribute to an invention, get a signed assignment from each one — there is no shortcut.
  • When acquiring a company or closing a funding round, verify that contractor assignments were actually executed in writing; a missing signature is a gap that can delay or kill the deal.
