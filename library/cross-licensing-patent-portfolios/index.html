Cross-Licensing Agreements: How Companies Trade Patent Rights Instead of Cash

Cross-licensing lets two companies grant each other rights to their respective patents, avoiding litigation and reducing the cost of doing business in overlapping technology spaces

2026-09-29 · cross-licensing · patent strategy · portfolio management · freedom to operate

When two companies each hold patents that the other needs, suing each other is often the worst outcome for both. Cross-licensing is the practical alternative: each side grants the other a license to some or all of its portfolio, and both walk away with freedom to operate in territory they otherwise could not touch. The arrangement is common in semiconductors, wireless communications, consumer electronics, and anywhere else where innovation builds heavily on prior innovation.

What a Cross-License Actually Is

A cross-license is a contract. Each party is simultaneously a licensor and a licensee. The agreement defines which patents are in scope, which products or fields of use those patents cover, and what — if anything — changes hands financially.

Because the two portfolios are rarely equal in size or value, pure royalty-free exchanges are less common than they appear from the outside. When one portfolio is significantly stronger, the weaker side often pays a balancing royalty — a cash payment or running royalty that compensates for the difference in value. This is sometimes called a "royalty balancing" or "net royalty" structure.

What Goes Into the Agreement

The key terms in any cross-license include:

  • Scope of patents licensed — all patents owned now, all patents ever issued to the party, or a defined subset by technology field or product category
  • Field of use — whether the license covers all products or only specific ones
  • Term — cross-licenses are often fixed in duration, which means they expire and must be renegotiated
  • Sublicensing rights — whether a licensee can extend its rights to subsidiaries or customers
  • Future patents — whether patents filed after the agreement is signed are automatically included
  • Royalty or balancing payment structure — if one party's portfolio is more valuable, how the difference is compensated

Each of these terms is a negotiation point, and the final agreement reflects the relative leverage each party brings to the table.

Why Companies Enter Cross-Licenses

The most common driver is mutual blocking. In dense technology spaces, two competitors may each hold patents that the other literally cannot design around. Neither can ship a product without risking an infringement claim. A cross-license resolves the standoff without litigation.

A second driver is cost avoidance. Patent litigation in the United States is expensive. Even a case that settles before trial can cost millions of dollars in legal fees. A cross-license converts that risk into a predictable agreement.

A third driver is market access. Particularly for companies entering a new technology segment, cross-licensing with an established player can provide rapid access to a broad patent portfolio that would otherwise take years and significant capital to design around or license individually.

When Cross-Licensing Raises Concerns

Cross-licenses are not always neutral. Antitrust regulators — including the Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission — pay attention to agreements between competitors, particularly when the deal includes provisions that restrict what either party can do outside the license. Patent pools, where multiple parties contribute patents and share a collective license, receive particularly close scrutiny because they can affect pricing and market entry. Nothing in this guide constitutes advice about any specific arrangement's antitrust exposure; that analysis requires counsel.

What Cross-Licenses Mean When You Are Reading a Portfolio Map

If you are analyzing a competitor's patent portfolio, a large portfolio does not automatically mean a large enforcement threat against you. That company may have existing cross-licenses with other major players — agreements that reduce the practical value of the portfolio as an offensive weapon, at least within certain fields.

Conversely, if your company is a startup without patents of your own, you have little to offer in a cross-license negotiation. This is one concrete reason why building a patent portfolio early matters: patents are negotiating currency. A company with no portfolio has no chips at the table if a larger competitor comes calling.

Publicly available patent data can show you who owns what and when applications were filed, but cross-license agreements themselves are private contracts and are generally not in the public record. The existence of a cross-license is sometimes disclosed in SEC filings or litigation documents, but the terms rarely are.

Practical Takeaways

  • A cross-license lets two companies exchange patent rights rather than fight over them, but the deal almost always has financial components when the portfolios are unequal in value
  • Key terms — scope, field of use, term, and sublicensing — define exactly what freedom each side actually receives
  • Cross-licenses expire; a company that relied on one and did not track the end date can find itself exposed when the agreement lapses
  • Startups with no patent portfolio have no leverage in a cross-license negotiation, which is a practical argument for filing early
  • Cross-license agreements are private, so a large competitor portfolio on a public database may be less threatening than it looks — or more, depending on what agreements already exist
  • Antitrust considerations apply to agreements between competitors; any cross-license or pool arrangement involving competing companies warrants review by qualified counsel
