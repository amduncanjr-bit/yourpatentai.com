How Patent Damages Are Calculated: What You Can Actually Recover

A plain-English explanation of how courts calculate patent damages, what the two main measures mean in practice, and why the numbers defendants face can be much larger than expected

2026-11-19 · patent litigation · patent damages · reasonable royalty · lost profits

When a court finds patent infringement, the question shifts immediately from liability to money. The damages calculation is where most of the real fighting happens in patent litigation, and the gap between what a patent owner expects to recover and what a court actually awards can be enormous. Understanding the two primary measures — lost profits and reasonable royalty — helps you evaluate both the risk of being sued and the potential value of enforcing your own patent.

The Statutory Floor

Under 35 U.S.C. § 284, a patent owner is entitled to damages adequate to compensate for the infringement, but in no event less than a reasonable royalty for the use made of the invention. That phrase "no event less than" is important: a reasonable royalty is the minimum, not the target. Patent owners who can prove more are entitled to more.

Damages run from the date the infringer had notice of the patent — either through actual knowledge or through proper marking of patented products — not necessarily from the date infringement began. If you sell a patented product without marking it, you can lose the right to collect damages for the period before you gave the infringer actual notice.

Lost Profits

Lost profits are what a patent owner would have earned but for the infringement. This is the more valuable measure when available, because it ties recovery to real business harm rather than a hypothetical license.

To recover lost profits, a patent owner typically needs to show:

  • Demand existed for the patented product
  • No acceptable non-infringing substitutes were available to customers
  • The patent owner had the manufacturing and marketing capacity to make the sales the infringer made
  • The amount of profit the patent owner would have earned on those sales

The third element — capacity — trips up smaller companies and startups. If you can show infringement but cannot demonstrate that you could have fulfilled the demand the infringer captured, your lost profits claim shrinks or disappears.

Where there are competing non-infringing substitutes in the market, lost profits calculations get complicated fast. Courts may apportion damages to reflect only the sales a patent owner would realistically have captured.

Reasonable Royalty

When a patent owner cannot prove lost profits, or for sales that fall outside a lost profits claim, the floor is a reasonable royalty. Courts construct a hypothetical negotiation: what royalty rate would a willing licensor and a willing licensee have agreed to at the moment infringement began, assuming both knew the patent was valid and infringed?

This hypothetical negotiation is not a real one. It is a legal framework courts use to anchor a number. Factors courts consider include:

  • Existing royalties the patent owner charges for comparable licenses
  • Royalties paid in the industry for similar technology
  • The nature of the patent and the scope of the license
  • The commercial relationship between the parties
  • The portion of the product's profit attributable to the patented feature versus everything else

That last factor — apportionment — is where defendants push hardest in software and technology cases. If the patented feature is one element of a complex product, the royalty base should reflect only the value of that feature, not the entire product's revenue. Courts have reinforced this requirement repeatedly, and defendants who can show that the patented element is a small piece of a large product often reduce exposure significantly.

What Drives the Numbers Up

Two things can multiply whatever base damages a court calculates.

Enhanced Damages

Section 284 also allows courts to increase damages up to three times the amount found or assessed. Enhanced damages are reserved for willful infringement — cases where the infringer knew about the patent and copied anyway without a credible legal basis for thinking the patent was invalid or not infringed. This is why companies that receive notice of a patent often seek legal opinions quickly.

Attorney Fees

In exceptional cases, the prevailing party can recover attorney fees under 35 U.S.C. § 285. Courts have discretion here, and fees are not automatic. But in cases involving egregious conduct or objectively weak litigation positions, fee awards add another layer of exposure.

Practical Takeaways

  • Damages begin accruing when the infringer has notice, so proper patent marking and direct notice letters both matter strategically
  • Lost profits require proving capacity to capture the sales you lost — startups without manufacturing scale often cannot meet this burden
  • Reasonable royalty is the floor, not a consolation prize; in high-volume markets it can still produce large verdicts
  • Apportionment is a defendant's best tool in complex product cases — pushing to isolate the value of the specific patented feature
  • Willful infringement can double or triple base damages, which is why a credible non-infringement or invalidity opinion is worth getting early
  • Attorney fees in exceptional cases add further risk on both sides, making early case evaluation important regardless of which side you are on
