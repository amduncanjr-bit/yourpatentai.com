Filing a Design Patent Application: What to Expect From Start to Grant

A plain-English walkthrough of how a design patent application is prepared, examined, and granted so inventors know what they are getting into before they file

2026-09-04 · design patents · patent prosecution · patent drawings · office actions

Design patents protect the ornamental appearance of a product, not how it works. But understanding what the protection covers is only half the battle — you also need to understand how to actually get one granted, because the process has quirks that catch first-time applicants off guard.

What Goes Into a Design Patent Application

A design patent application is much shorter than a utility patent application, but that brevity is deceptive. The drawings are the application. The USPTO requires a complete set of views — typically front, back, left side, right side, top, and bottom, plus a perspective view — rendered in precise line-drawing format that follows strict rules about line weight, shading, and broken-line conventions.

The Single Claim

A design application contains exactly one claim. It reads something like: "The ornamental design for a [product name], as shown and described." That sentence does almost no work on its own. The drawings define the scope of protection entirely. Every line you include in solid form is claimed. Every feature you want to exclude — because it is functional, background context, or something you want to leave open for competitors to design around — should be shown in broken lines.

This is where applicants frequently make costly mistakes. Over-claiming by showing too much in solid lines narrows your protection unnecessarily. Under-claiming by omitting key features leaves the design vulnerable to easy design-arounds.

Drawings Are Worth Hiring a Professional For

The USPTO will reject applications with non-compliant drawings. Corrections require a formal amendment and delay prosecution. Professional patent illustrators who specialize in design patent drawings understand the rules and produce work that survives examination. This is one area where cutting costs tends to cost more time in the end.

The Examination Process

Once filed, the application is assigned to a design patent examiner. Design patent prosecution is generally faster than utility patent prosecution. The USPTO's own data shows design patents often issue within about 18 to 24 months of filing, and many issue faster, though timelines vary.

What Examiners Look For

The examiner evaluates two main questions. First, is the design novel — is there prior art showing the same or very similar appearance? Second, is the design ornamental, meaning it is not purely dictated by function? A design whose every feature is required to make the product work cannot be protected by a design patent.

The prior art search for design patents focuses on visual similarity. An examiner will apply what courts call the "ordinary observer" test: would an ordinary observer, familiar with the prior art, confuse the claimed design with an earlier one? If yes, the application faces a rejection.

Office Actions in Design Cases

Design applications do receive office actions, most commonly for drawing informalities or prior art rejections. A drawing informality is usually fixable with corrected sheets. A prior art rejection requires an argument — typically distinguishing your design visually from the cited reference — or an amendment to the drawings to narrow scope.

You have a limited number of responses before the application goes abandoned, so responses to office actions should be taken seriously even though the overall application is short.

After Grant: Maintenance and Term

A design patent that issues today has a term of 15 years from the grant date. There are no maintenance fees. Once it issues, you own it for 15 years without paying anything further to keep it alive. That is a meaningful practical difference from utility patents, which require maintenance fees at 3.5, 7.5, and 11.5 years post-grant.

Marking Your Products

Once your design patent issues, you can mark products with the patent number. Proper marking matters for damages purposes — under U.S. patent law, you generally cannot recover damages for infringement that occurred before an infringer had actual notice of the patent if your products were not marked. Many companies use virtual marking, placing a URL on the product that links to a page listing applicable patents.

Practical Takeaways

  • The drawings define your protection — invest in professional patent illustrations and think carefully about what to show in solid versus broken lines before filing.
  • Design patent prosecution is typically faster than utility patent prosecution, but non-compliant drawings are a common source of delay.
  • Prior art rejections focus on visual similarity from the perspective of an ordinary observer, not functional differences.
  • Design patents have a 15-year term from grant with no maintenance fees, making them low-cost to hold once granted.
  • Mark your products with the patent number after grant to preserve your ability to collect damages from the date of infringement rather than only from the date of actual notice.
