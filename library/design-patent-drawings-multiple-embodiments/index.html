Multiple Embodiments in a Design Patent Application: What You Can Show and What It Costs You

A single design patent application can include more than one embodiment of your design, but the rules governing what qualifies and what you give up are worth understanding before you file

2026-10-26 · design patents · embodiments · patent drawings · restriction requirement

A design patent protects the ornamental appearance of a product — and sometimes a single product ships in more than one visual form. You might offer a device with a round corner variant and a flat corner variant, or a UI icon that appears in two slightly different aspect ratios. The question is whether you can capture both in one application, and if so, how.

What an Embodiment Is in This Context

An embodiment, in design patent practice, is a distinct visual version of the same design concept. The USPTO allows multiple embodiments in a single design application, but only under a specific condition: all embodiments must present a single inventive concept. That phrase has practical meaning. The embodiments must share the same overall visual impression, differing only in minor ways that would not require separate searching or examination.

If your two versions look substantially alike to an ordinary observer — same basic shape, same ornamental elements, minor proportional variation — they likely qualify. If they look like two genuinely different designs that happen to serve the same function, the USPTO will issue a restriction requirement and make you choose one, or file separate applications for each.

How the Drawings Work Across Embodiments

Each embodiment gets its own complete set of views. If your single-embodiment application requires seven views (front, rear, left, right, top, bottom, perspective), then two embodiments mean fourteen views, each set labeled to make clear which embodiment it depicts. The drawings must be consistent within each embodiment and clearly distinguished from the other.

Broken lines remain available. Portions of the article shown in broken lines are not part of the claimed design and can be used to show context or environmental structure that differs between embodiments without affecting the scope of the claim.

What You Gain and What You Give Up

The Upside of Multiple Embodiments

Filing one application with two embodiments instead of two separate applications saves you one set of filing fees and keeps prosecution unified. You get a single prosecution history, a single grant date, and a single patent number covering both visual versions. If a competitor copies either version, you have one document to assert.

For products that genuinely iterate slightly between design cycles — a hardware button that moved a few millimeters, a bezel edge that changed radius — embodiments give you a way to document both without doubling your filing budget.

The Downside You Should Understand

The single inventive concept requirement is enforced by examiners, and they do restrict applications they view as overcrowded. If the examiner issues a restriction, you elect one embodiment and prosecute it. You can file a divisional application for the non-elected embodiment, but now you have paid two sets of filing fees anyway, and the divisional will not share the original filing date benefit in the same seamless way as a continuation.

More importantly, prosecution history applies to the entire application. Arguments you make to distinguish prior art during examination will limit the scope of every embodiment in the grant. You cannot argue your way around a prior art reference for one embodiment while preserving broader scope for another.

Practical Situations Where Multiple Embodiments Come Up

  • Consumer hardware with finish or edge variants. A device sold in two chassis geometries that are closely related visually.
  • UI icons or screen displays. A design that appears at two standard resolutions or in light mode and dark mode, where the underlying shape is identical.
  • Packaging with minor structural variation. A box or container offered in two sizes where the proportional difference is small and the ornamental treatment is the same.
  • Wearables. A band or casing that ships with a slightly different clasp profile across two product lines.

In each case, the threshold question is the same: would an ordinary observer see one design concept expressed in two similar ways, or two distinct designs? If the answer is genuinely the former, embodiments are available. If there is real doubt, separate applications give you cleaner prosecution and independent scope.

Practical Takeaways

  • Multiple embodiments are permitted in a single design application only when all embodiments share a single inventive concept — minor visual differences, not distinct designs.
  • Each embodiment requires its own complete set of views, properly labeled and consistent.
  • One application with two embodiments costs one filing fee upfront, but a restriction requirement can force you into divisional filings and eliminate that savings.
  • Prosecution history arguments bind all embodiments in the application — you cannot compartmentalize concessions by embodiment.
  • When two versions of your design are meaningfully different visually, separate applications from the start give you cleaner scope and independent prosecution records.
  • If you are uncertain whether your variants qualify as a single inventive concept, that question is worth resolving before you file, not after an examiner restricts the application.
