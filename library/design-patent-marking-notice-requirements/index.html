Design Patent Marking: What You Must Put on Your Product and What Happens If You Don't

Design patent marking rules determine whether you can collect damages for past infringement, and getting them wrong costs you money you cannot recover later.

2026-11-21 · design patents · patent marking · damages · virtual marking

When you receive a design patent, the grant itself does not automatically protect your financial interests in litigation. What you do next — specifically, how you notify the public that your product is patented — determines how far back a damages award can reach. This is not a technicality. It is a rule with real financial consequences that many founders and product teams miss entirely.

What Patent Marking Actually Does

Patent marking is the mechanism Congress created to put the public on constructive notice that a product is protected. Under 35 U.S.C. § 287, a patent owner who makes, sells, or offers for sale a patented article must mark it with the patent number. If you do not mark, you generally cannot recover damages for infringement that occurred before you gave the infringer actual notice — meaning you sent a letter, filed a lawsuit, or otherwise directly told them.

For design patents, the marking obligation works the same way as for utility patents. The practical effect is straightforward: an infringer who copies your design and sells competing products for two years before you file suit may owe you nothing for that entire period if your product was never marked.

How to Mark a Design Patent Correctly

The statute requires marking the article itself with the word "Patent" or the abbreviation "Pat." followed by the patent number. For design patents, the number format follows the grant — for example, "Pat. D1,000,000" or "US D1,000,000."

Physical Products

For physical goods, marking goes directly on the product when practicable. If the product itself is too small or the material makes direct marking impractical, the marking can appear on the packaging. Courts have scrutinized what "practicable" means, so if you are skipping product marking in favor of packaging, there should be a genuine reason — not just convenience.

Virtual Marking

Since the America Invents Act, patent owners can use virtual marking instead of printing numbers on every unit. Virtual marking means putting the word "Patent" or "Pat." on the product along with a URL that associates the product with the patent number. The webpage at that URL must be publicly accessible and kept current. If your patent number changes — because a continuation issues, for example — you update the webpage rather than retooling your packaging.

Virtual marking is especially useful for consumer products where patent portfolios evolve and re-marking physical inventory is expensive. The tradeoff is that you must maintain the webpage. A broken link or a page that no longer lists the relevant patent can undermine your marking argument.

What You Cannot Do

False marking — putting a patent number on a product that is not covered by that patent, or on a product after the patent expires — carries its own legal risk. Competitive false marking claims can be brought by the government or in some circumstances by private parties. Do not mark products with patents that do not cover them, and remove or update marking when patents expire.

The Actual Notice Alternative

If you never marked your product, you can still recover damages — but only from the date you provided actual notice to the specific infringer. Actual notice requires an affirmative communication that identifies the patent and the accused product or activity. A general cease-and-desist letter that does not name the patent or the infringing product may not qualify. Filing a complaint in federal court has consistently been treated as actual notice, but that starts the clock only from the filing date.

This means that every month you sell an unmarked product is a month of potential damages you may never recover, regardless of how long the infringement has been occurring.

When Marking Becomes Complicated

If you license your design patent to manufacturers or distributors, your licensees' products must also be marked. A patent owner who fails to ensure that licensees mark can lose the marking benefit even if the patent owner's own products are properly marked. License agreements should include an express marking obligation on the licensee, and you should verify compliance.

If you have multiple design patents covering different ornamental features of the same product, each applicable patent number should be included in the marking. Courts have not required exhaustive precision in every edge case, but erring toward completeness is safer than leaving numbers off.

Practical Takeaways

  • Mark your product with the design patent number as soon as the patent issues — waiting costs you damages for every day of unmarked sales.
  • Virtual marking is legally valid and easier to maintain for products with evolving patent coverage; keep the referenced webpage accurate and accessible.
  • Licensees must also mark — build that obligation into every license agreement and verify it.
  • Actual notice (a letter or lawsuit) can substitute for marking, but it starts the damages clock only from that date, not before.
  • Never mark a product with a patent that does not cover it, and remove marking when a patent expires.
  • If marking is impractical on the product itself, packaging is an accepted alternative — but document why direct marking was not feasible.
