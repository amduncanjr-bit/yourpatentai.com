What an Invention Assignment Clause Actually Covers — and Where It Stops

Invention assignment clauses in employment agreements are broader than most employees realize, but they have legal limits that matter if you build something outside work.

2026-10-20 · invention assignment · employment contracts · ip ownership · employee inventors

When you sign an employment agreement, the invention assignment clause is easy to skim past. It looks like standard boilerplate, and in many ways it is — but what it actually transfers to your employer can surprise you later, especially if you write code, build tools, or develop methods outside your day job. Understanding where these clauses reach, and where they legally cannot reach, is something every technical employee and founder should know before signing.

What the Clause Typically Says

Most invention assignment clauses require you to assign to your employer any invention you create that meets one or more of the following conditions:

  • It was made using the employer's equipment, supplies, facilities, or trade secrets
  • It was developed during work hours, even partially
  • It relates to the employer's current or reasonably anticipated business
  • It results from work you performed for the employer

Some agreements go further and include a broad catch-all covering anything you invent during the term of your employment, full stop. The breadth of that language matters enormously, because an assignment clause transfers ownership automatically — you don't sign a separate document each time an invention is created. If the clause covers it, it belongs to the employer from the moment it exists.

The "Relates to Our Business" Problem

The most expansive language in these clauses is usually the phrase that captures inventions "relating to the company's current or reasonably anticipated business." For a large technology company, that phrase can cover an enormous range of subject matter. If your employer sells cloud infrastructure software and you build a personal productivity app in your spare time, the employer may argue your invention falls within anticipated business. Whether that argument succeeds depends on how the clause is written and, in litigation, how a court interprets it.

Where State Law Draws the Line

Several states have enacted statutes that limit what employers can require employees to assign. California, Delaware, Illinois, Minnesota, North Carolina, and Washington each have versions of this protection. The specifics vary by state, but the general rule is that an employer cannot claim ownership of an invention that meets all of the following:

  • You developed it entirely on your own time
  • You used no employer equipment, supplies, facilities, or trade secrets
  • It does not relate to the employer's business or anticipated business
  • It does not result from work you performed for the employer

If all four conditions are met, the invention is yours — regardless of what the assignment clause says. These statutes override conflicting contract language. An agreement that tries to grab inventions beyond these boundaries is unenforceable to that extent in states with such protections.

Not every state has this protection. If you live and work in a state without a limiting statute, a broad contractual clause may be fully enforceable as written.

The Post-Employment Window

Many agreements also include a provision covering inventions created within a specified period after your employment ends — often six months to a year. The theory is that inventions conceived while you were employed but completed afterward should still belong to the employer. These clauses are legitimate but should be scrutinized. If you are leaving a job to start a company, the inventions that form your new product's foundation need to be cleanly outside this window, or you may face a dispute about ownership before you ever file a patent.

What This Means Before You File a Patent

If you file a patent application on an invention that is arguably covered by a prior assignment clause, the USPTO will still accept the application — it does not police employment agreements. But if the employer later discovers the filing, it may assert ownership. That creates a chain-of-title problem that can cloud the patent's value, affect licensing, and complicate any future investment or acquisition.

Before filing, it is worth asking: Does this invention fall within the four corners of what I assigned? Was any part of it developed using employer resources? Does it relate to a market my employer is in or has discussed entering? These are factual questions, and the answers shape your exposure.

Prior inventions schedules — the exhibits many employment agreements include where you list work you want excluded from the assignment — are the appropriate moment to carve out anything you want to keep. If you skipped that step when you were hired, you cannot easily go back and add to it later.

Practical Takeaways

  • Invention assignment clauses transfer ownership automatically; there is no separate signing required each time
  • "Relates to our business" is the broadest and most contested phrase in these clauses — take it seriously
  • Several states, including California and Illinois, limit what employers can claim by statute; know whether your state is one of them
  • Post-employment invention windows are real and can reach into your startup's founding technology
  • Prior inventions schedules at the time of hire are your best contractual tool for protecting existing and planned personal work
  • If you have a specific situation, review the actual clause with an attorney before filing anything — a chain-of-title dispute after grant is far more expensive than clarity beforehand
