Joint Inventorship in Employment Settings: Who Gets Credit and What It Actually Costs You

When two or more people contribute to a patented invention in a workplace setting, the rules for who qualifies as a joint inventor have real consequences for ownership, licensing rights, and what happens if the inventorship record is wrong

2026-11-02 · joint inventorship · invention assignment · co-ownership · employment agreements

Most people assume that whoever wrote the code, drew the design, or built the prototype is the inventor. But patent law defines inventorship by contribution to the claimed invention — not by effort, employment title, or who signed the project charter. In a workplace where multiple people touch a product, that distinction creates problems that surface months or years after a patent issues.

What Joint Inventorship Actually Means

Under U.S. patent law, a joint inventor is someone who contributed to the conception of at least one claim in the patent. Conception means the formation of a definite and permanent idea of the complete and operative invention. That is a specific legal standard, not a common-sense one.

What does not qualify someone as a joint inventor:

  • Following instructions given by the actual inventor
  • Doing the experimental or engineering work that reduced the idea to practice
  • Suggesting the general problem to be solved
  • Contributing to features that appear in the specification but are not reflected in any claim

Two people can be joint inventors even if they never worked in the same room, contributed unequal amounts, or worked at different times. What matters is whether each person contributed to the conception of at least one claim as it was ultimately allowed.

Why Employment Complicates This

In a solo-inventor scenario, the ownership question is relatively clean: the inventor owns the patent, subject to any assignment agreement. Joint inventorship in an employment context multiplies the complications.

Each Joint Inventor Is a Co-Owner by Default

Under 35 U.S.C. § 262, each co-owner of a U.S. patent can make, use, sell, and license the patent without the consent of the other co-owners and without accounting to them. That means if two employees jointly invent something and only one of them has signed an invention assignment agreement, the unassigned co-owner retains independent rights — including the right to license the patent to a competitor.

This is not a theoretical concern. Employers who carefully collect invention assignments from full-time employees sometimes fail to get them from contractors, consultants, part-time staff, or employees hired before the assignment policy was in place. If any one of those people contributed to the conception of a claim, they are a co-owner.

The Assignment Agreement Only Covers What Was Assigned

An invention assignment clause obligates the signing employee to assign their interest to the employer. It does not automatically pull in the interest of anyone else who contributed to the invention. If a co-inventor never signed an assignment, the employer gets only the assigning inventor's undivided interest — not the whole patent.

Misjoinder and Nonjoinder

Listing the wrong people as inventors — either by including someone who did not contribute to a claim, or by omitting someone who did — is an error called misjoinder or nonjoinder. Either one can be corrected at the USPTO through a formal inventorship correction proceeding, but the process requires declarations and, if the error was not made with deceptive intent, the patent remains valid. Errors made with deceptive intent carry much more serious consequences and can render a patent unenforceable.

In litigation, inventorship is frequently attacked as a way to challenge patent validity. A defendant who can show that a true co-inventor was left off the patent — particularly one who never assigned their rights — has a powerful argument that the patent is unenforceable or that the plaintiff lacks standing to sue alone.

What Employers and Inventors Should Watch For

For Employers

  • Audit assignment agreements whenever a new person joins a project, including contractors and collaborators at partner companies
  • Identify everyone who contributed technically to a project before a patent application is filed, not after
  • If a joint inventor is an employee of a different company, determine early whether that company will be a co-applicant or whether rights need to be sorted by contract before filing

For Individual Inventors

  • Understand that your name on a patent does not mean you own it — your assignment agreement may have already transferred your interest to your employer
  • If you are omitted from a patent that covers your contributions, you have legal options to seek correction, but timing and documentation matter
  • If you contributed to a claim but your employer disputes it, that is a factual and legal question that can be contested

Practical Takeaways

  • Joint inventorship is determined by contribution to claims, not by who worked hardest or longest on a project
  • Each co-owner of a U.S. patent can independently license it unless all co-owners have assigned their interests
  • A single unassigned co-inventor can undermine an employer's exclusive control of a patent
  • Inventorship errors can be corrected at the USPTO, but errors made with deceptive intent can make a patent unenforceable
  • Employers should collect invention assignments from everyone who touches a project, including contractors, before filing
  • Individual contributors who believe they were improperly excluded from a patent should document their contributions carefully and seek qualified legal advice
