Noncompete Clauses and Patent Rights: How Post-Employment Restrictions Interact With What You Invented

Noncompete agreements and invention assignment clauses often overlap in ways that can limit what you build, file, or commercialize after you leave a job

2026-11-15 · noncompete · invention assignment · employment contracts · post-employment

When you leave an employer, two separate legal frameworks follow you out the door: the noncompete clause in your employment agreement and the invention assignment clause in the same document. Most employees treat these as one problem. They are not. They operate differently, they enforce differently, and failing to understand where one ends and the other begins can cost you patent rights you assumed were yours.

What a Noncompete Actually Restricts

A noncompete clause limits where you can work and what kind of work you can do — it does not, by itself, say anything about who owns inventions. It typically bars you from joining a competitor or starting a competing business within a defined geography and time window after your departure.

The practical patent problem is timing. If you conceive of an invention during the noncompete period and that invention falls within your former employer's field of business, the noncompete may not transfer ownership to them — but a simultaneous invention assignment clause might. Courts and practitioners generally analyze these two provisions separately, but they interact in predictable ways:

  • A noncompete can prevent you from commercializing an invention even if you personally own it, because commercializing it may constitute competing.
  • An assignment clause can transfer ownership of an invention you conceived after your departure if you used the employer's confidential information, tools, or resources.
  • Filing a patent application on a restricted invention during a noncompete period can create written evidence that you were competing, depending on how the clause is drafted.

How State Law Shapes the Analysis

Enforceability of noncompetes varies dramatically by state. California, for example, renders most noncompetes void as a matter of statute for employees. Minnesota and Oklahoma have adopted similar rules. Other states — including Florida — enforce them broadly if the employer can show a legitimate business interest.

This matters for patent filings because the same invention may be freely commercializable if you are in one state and completely restricted if you are in another. The state whose law governs your agreement is often not the state where you live or work. Employment contracts routinely include choice-of-law provisions that specify a different state's law will apply. That clause controls — with some limits — even if you later relocate.

What to Check in Your Agreement

  • Which state's law governs the contract?
  • How is "competitive activity" defined? Does it cover patent filing, licensing, or both?
  • Is there a separate "garden leave" or "notice period" clause that extends assignment obligations beyond your last day?
  • Does the invention assignment clause separately cover inventions conceived within some period after departure?

The Specific Risk Around Patent Applications

Patent applications are public documents once published. A patent published 18 months after filing shows the invention date in the prosecution file wrapper, your name as inventor, and the technology. If your former employer believes that invention belongs to them — under the assignment clause, the noncompete, or both — a published application hands them a roadmap for a lawsuit.

This is not theoretical. Employers do monitor published applications from former employees, particularly in competitive technology sectors. The risk is highest when:

  • The invention is in the same technical field as your former role
  • You used confidential information or company-developed tools during development
  • You filed quickly after departure, suggesting conception may have occurred before your last day
  • Your assignment clause includes a "trailer clause" extending obligations for six to twelve months post-employment

Trailer Clauses Deserve Special Attention

A trailer clause — sometimes called a holdover clause — expressly captures inventions conceived after employment ends, within a fixed window, if the invention relates to the employer's business or was developed using their confidential information. Some states limit or void trailer clauses; others enforce them. Reading your agreement for this language before you file anything is essential.

Practical Takeaways

  • Noncompete clauses and invention assignment clauses are separate provisions with different legal effects — read both before you file or commercialize anything after leaving a job.
  • The state whose law governs your agreement controls enforceability, and that state may not be where you live or work.
  • A published patent application is discoverable by your former employer and can serve as evidence in a dispute over ownership or competitive activity.
  • Trailer clauses can extend your former employer's ownership claims past your last day of employment — look for them explicitly.
  • If your noncompete bars competitive activity and you are unsure whether filing a patent application qualifies, that ambiguity is worth resolving before you file, not after.
  • Nothing in this guide is legal advice; the interaction between your specific agreement, your state's law, and your invention's facts requires a qualified attorney to evaluate.
