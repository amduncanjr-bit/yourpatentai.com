Anticipation vs. Obviousness: How the USPTO Rejects Claims and What the Difference Means for You

Understanding the difference between anticipation and obviousness rejections helps applicants respond more effectively and avoid giving up more claim scope than necessary

2026-10-10 · patent prosecution · office actions · anticipation · obviousness

When a patent examiner rejects your claims, the rejection almost always falls into one of two legal categories: anticipation or obviousness. These words sound similar, but they work differently, they require different responses, and confusing them is one of the most common ways applicants argue past the actual problem during prosecution.

Anticipation: Every Element, One Reference

An anticipation rejection under 35 U.S.C. § 102 means the examiner believes a single prior art reference already discloses every element of your claim. Every element. If even one element is missing from that reference, anticipation fails.

That specificity is important. It means when you receive a § 102 rejection, your first job is to read the cited reference carefully against each element of the rejected claim, one by one. Ask:

  • Does this reference actually disclose that element, or is the examiner inferring it?
  • Is the examiner reading an element onto the reference by stretching the plain meaning?
  • Is there an element the reference simply does not disclose at all?

If you find a genuine gap, you can argue it directly. Your argument does not need to be long — it needs to be precise. Point to the specific element, point to the specific absence in the reference, and explain why the examiner's reading does not hold up.

If the reference does disclose everything and there is no credible argument otherwise, amendment is usually more productive than argument. Narrowing the claim to add a limitation the reference does not teach eliminates the § 102 rejection — though it may create prosecution history you will need to manage carefully.

When the Dates Matter

§ 102 also has a temporal dimension. A reference can only anticipate if it qualifies as prior art under the applicable date rules. Under the America Invents Act, the critical date for most applications is the effective filing date. If a reference was published after your priority date, it cannot anticipate your claims. Checking dates sounds basic, but it is a step applicants sometimes skip under time pressure.

Obviousness: Combining References to Fill the Gaps

An obviousness rejection under 35 U.S.C. § 103 is more flexible and, frankly, harder to defeat. Here the examiner is not saying one reference discloses everything. The examiner is saying that a person of ordinary skill in the relevant field would have found it obvious to combine two or more references — or to modify a single reference — to arrive at your claimed invention.

The examiner must provide a reason why a skilled person would have made that combination. That reason cannot just be the fact that your invention exists. Look at what rationale the examiner offered:

  • Is the combination actually logical, or is it driven by hindsight from knowing your invention?
  • Do the references teach away from each other — meaning one reference discourages the modification the examiner is proposing?
  • Does the combination actually produce the claimed result, or does something get lost in the merge?

Secondary Considerations

Obviousness arguments are not limited to attacking the examiner's combination logic. The law recognizes objective evidence that cuts against an obviousness finding. These are sometimes called secondary considerations or objective indicia of non-obviousness, and they include things like commercial success of the claimed invention, long-felt but unresolved need in the field, failure of others to achieve the same result, and unexpected results. These are real arguments with real weight, but they require factual support — declarations, sales data, or contemporaneous evidence. Conclusory statements do not move examiners.

How the Two Rejections Often Appear Together

Examiners frequently issue both § 102 and § 103 rejections in the same office action, sometimes against the same claims and sometimes against different ones. A common pattern: the independent claim gets a § 103 rejection combining two references, while a dependent claim gets a § 102 rejection from a single reference that happens to disclose the narrower limitation. Read each rejection separately and respond to each on its own terms. A single argument addressed to both usually serves neither well.

Practical Takeaways

  • A § 102 rejection requires one reference to disclose every claim element; if any element is missing, the rejection does not hold.
  • A § 103 rejection requires the examiner to provide a reason to combine references, not just proof that combination is possible.
  • Check the publication date of every cited reference against your effective filing date before drafting any response.
  • Hindsight bias — building a case for obviousness only because the invention is known — is a legitimate argument but requires careful framing.
  • Secondary considerations like long-felt need or unexpected results can defeat a § 103 rejection but need evidentiary support, not just assertion.
  • When amendment is necessary, add limitations that are clearly supported by your specification to avoid introducing new matter or losing priority.
