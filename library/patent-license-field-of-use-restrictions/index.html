Field-of-Use Restrictions in Patent Licenses: What They Limit and Why It Matters

Field-of-use restrictions in patent licenses define exactly where your rights begin and end, and missing what they exclude can expose your product to infringement claims you never anticipated

2026-10-21 · patent licensing · due diligence · field of use · license agreements

A patent license that looks broad on the surface can carry hidden limits that carve out whole product categories, geographies, or customer segments from the rights you think you received. Field-of-use restrictions are one of the most common—and most underread—clauses in technology patent licenses. Understanding how they work before you sign, or before you acquire a company that has already signed, can prevent a costly surprise later.

What a Field-of-Use Restriction Actually Is

A field-of-use restriction is a contractual provision that limits the scope of a patent license to a defined category of activity. Instead of granting the licensee the right to use the patent for any purpose, the licensor grants rights only within a specified field—a particular industry, application type, distribution channel, or end-user class.

For example, a license might cover "use of the patented method in consumer mobile applications" but exclude enterprise software, medical devices, or automotive systems. If your product later expands into one of those excluded areas, the license you already hold does not cover it. You would need a separate license—or you could be infringing.

Field-of-use restrictions are generally enforceable under U.S. patent law. Courts have long recognized a patent owner's right to divide the bundle of rights a patent represents and grant each piece independently. This is sometimes called the principle of partial assignment or partial license, and it gives IP owners significant flexibility in monetizing their portfolios.

Why These Restrictions Show Up and What Forms They Take

Licensors use field-of-use restrictions for several legitimate business reasons:

  • Revenue segmentation. A patent owner may license the same patent to multiple parties, each in a different field, collecting royalties from each without the licensees competing with one another.
  • Protecting downstream deals. A licensor may have already granted exclusive rights in one field and needs to exclude that field from every subsequent license it grants.
  • Retaining optionality. A licensor may want to enter a particular market itself later, so it deliberately excludes that field from outgoing licenses.

Field-of-use definitions can be drafted narrowly or broadly, and the drafting choices matter enormously. Common formulations include:

  • By industry vertical (healthcare, financial services, education)
  • By technology type (wireless transmission, image processing, machine learning inference)
  • By distribution method (direct-to-consumer only, OEM only, SaaS delivery only)
  • By geography combined with use (U.S. consumer market only)
  • By customer class (individual users, not enterprise accounts with more than 500 seats)

Ambiguous definitions are where disputes concentrate. If the license says "mobile applications" and you later ship a tablet-optimized version or a Progressive Web App, it may not be obvious whether your product falls inside or outside the licensed field.

Due Diligence Checklist for Field-of-Use Provisions

Whether you are reviewing a license your company holds, evaluating a target company's IP in an acquisition, or negotiating a new license agreement, focus on these questions:

Scope of the Defined Field

- Is the field defined by reference to a specific product, an industry, a technology, or a customer type—and does that definition match how your product actually works? - Does the definition use terms of art that have a specific meaning in the relevant industry or in the patent claims themselves? - Is there a catch-all or residual clause that could sweep in unanticipated uses?

What Is Explicitly Excluded

- Does the license carve out any fields that your current or planned product roadmap touches? - Are there exclusions tied to regulatory categories—such as FDA-regulated devices or HIPAA-covered entities—that could affect a product pivot?

Sublicensing and Transfer Rights

- Can you sublicense within the permitted field? If your business model involves licensing your platform to others, sublicensing rights matter. - Does the field-of-use restriction survive a change of control? Some licenses automatically narrow or terminate if the licensee is acquired.

Interaction with Exclusivity

- If the license is exclusive within a field, understand whether the licensor retained the right to practice the patent itself in that field—some exclusive licenses carve out the licensor.

Practical Takeaways

  • Read the field-of-use definition against your actual product and your three-year roadmap, not just what your company does today.
  • Treat undefined or ambiguous terms as negotiation points; request a definition that covers foreseeable product expansion.
  • In acquisition due diligence, map every inbound patent license against the target's full product portfolio to find gaps between licensed fields and actual use.
  • If a license predates a significant product pivot by the target company, verify that the pivot did not move the product outside the licensed field.
  • Sublicensing and change-of-control provisions interact with field-of-use clauses in ways that are easy to miss; review them together, not in isolation.
  • When in doubt about whether a product use falls inside a licensed field, get a written clarification from the licensor before you ship—conduct that confirms scope can matter later.
