Patent Marking for Mobile Apps: What the Law Requires When There Is No Physical Product

Patent marking rules for physical products do not translate cleanly to software and mobile apps, and misunderstanding what is required can cost you damages you would otherwise recover

2026-11-24 · patent marking · mobile apps · virtual marking · method claims

Patent marking is how you tell the world that a product is covered by a patent — and failing to do it correctly limits the damages you can collect if someone infringes. That rule is straightforward for a physical product you stamp or label. For a mobile app, where there is nothing to stamp, the analysis is less obvious but no less important.

Why Marking Matters

Under 35 U.S.C. § 287, a patent owner who makes, offers for sale, or sells a patented article must mark it with the patent number, or provide actual notice of infringement to the defendant. If you do neither, you can only recover damages from the date you gave the defendant actual notice — not from the date infringement began. For a mobile app that may have been copied for years before you filed suit, the difference can be substantial.

The statute uses the word "article," which courts have interpreted to mean a tangible product. If your patent covers only a method — a series of steps performed by the app — the marking requirement does not apply, because there is no article to mark. Method-only patents are therefore exempt. But if your patent has any product or system claims, even one, marking becomes relevant.

Virtual Marking and Where to Put It

Congress amended the marking statute in 2011 to allow virtual marking. Instead of displaying the patent number directly, you can post a URL that links to a page associating your product with the applicable patent numbers. The URL itself must appear on the product or its packaging.

For a mobile app, courts and practitioners generally treat the following as acceptable locations for a virtual marking notice:

  • The app's "About" screen or settings menu, where a persistent notice links to a marking page
  • The app store listing, though this alone is likely insufficient without an in-app notice as well
  • A dedicated URL on your website, such as yourcompany.com/patents, that lists the product and patent numbers together

The marking page the URL points to must actually associate each patent number with the product it covers. A generic list of company patents with no connection to specific products does not satisfy the requirement.

Keeping the Marking Page Current

One practical obligation that trips up founders is maintenance. When a new patent issues that covers an already-shipped app, you must update your marking page promptly. When a patent expires or is invalidated, it should come off the page. The law does not demand instantaneous updates, but courts look at whether the patent owner made reasonable efforts to keep the information accurate. Stale or incomplete pages undermine the value of the notice you are trying to establish.

Method Claims and the Marking Exception

If every claim in your patent is a method claim — claim 1 recites "a method comprising" steps performed by software, with no claim directed to a system, device, or apparatus — then § 287 does not require you to mark at all. The rationale is that you cannot stamp a process.

This distinction matters when you draft or amend claims. A patent that includes both method claims and system claims is subject to the marking requirement for the system claims. If you want to avoid the administrative burden of marking entirely, a method-only claim strategy can accomplish that, but it trades one complexity for another: method claims have their own enforcement and scope considerations that are worth evaluating separately.

False Marking

Marking a product with a patent number that does not actually cover it — or continuing to mark after the patent expires — can expose you to false marking liability under 35 U.S.C. § 292. The risk is not purely theoretical. Keeping your marking page accurate protects you from both failure-to-mark problems on one side and false marking exposure on the other.

What "Covers" the App Means

A patent "covers" a product when at least one claim reads on it — meaning the product practices every element of the claim. If your app has changed significantly since the patent issued, it is worth confirming that the claims still read on the current version before continuing to mark. This is not a one-time analysis; it should be revisited when the app is materially updated.

Practical Takeaways

  • If your patent has any product or system claims, the marking requirement applies to your app even though it is software.
  • Virtual marking — a URL in the app pointing to a page that associates your patent numbers with your product — satisfies the statute.
  • Keep your marking page current; adding new patents and removing expired ones is an ongoing obligation, not a one-time task.
  • Method-only patents are exempt from the marking requirement, but that exemption should be a deliberate drafting choice, not an accident.
  • Failing to mark limits your damages to the period after you gave the infringer actual notice, which can eliminate years of recoverable harm.
  • False marking carries its own liability; accuracy on both ends matters.
