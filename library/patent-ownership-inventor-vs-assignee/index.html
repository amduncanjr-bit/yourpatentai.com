Inventor vs. Assignee: Who Actually Owns the Patent and Why It Matters in a Deal

Understanding the difference between an inventor and an assignee tells you who legally holds patent rights and where ownership gaps can derail a transaction

2026-11-03 · due diligence · patent ownership · assignment · inventorship

When a patent issues, the USPTO prints two names that are not the same thing: the inventor and the applicant or assignee. Buyers, investors, and licensees routinely treat these as interchangeable. They are not, and confusing them is one of the most common reasons a patent portfolio turns out to be worth less than expected after a deal closes.

What the Inventor Record Actually Means

Inventorship is a legal status, not a corporate title. The inventor is the person — always a human being — who conceived the claimed invention. Inventorship is determined claim by claim: if a person contributed to at least one claim that issued, they are an inventor. If they did not, they should not be listed, regardless of how senior they are or how much work they did on the product.

The inventor record on a granted patent is public and does not automatically transfer when a company changes hands. What the inventor holds, absent a valid written assignment, is an undivided ownership interest in the patent. In the United States, any co-owner can license the patent without the consent of the other co-owners and without sharing the proceeds. That is not a hypothetical risk — it is a default rule under U.S. patent law.

Why Incorrect Inventorship Creates Problems

If a person is listed as an inventor who did not actually contribute to any claim — or if a genuine contributor was left off — the patent can be challenged on that basis. Honest mistakes can often be corrected at the USPTO, but deliberate misjoinder can render a patent unenforceable. During due diligence, a mismatch between the people named on the patent and the people who actually built the technology is a flag worth investigating.

What the Assignee Record Means

The assignee is the entity — a company, an LLC, a trust — that has received the inventor's ownership rights through a written assignment. An assignment must be in writing to be effective under U.S. law. Oral agreements and general employment relationships do not automatically transfer patent rights, even when an employee was hired specifically to invent.

Assignments are recorded at the USPTO, but recording is not required for the transfer to be valid between the parties. Recording matters for a different reason: a later purchaser who pays value and has no notice of an earlier unrecorded assignment can take priority over that earlier assignee. This is why chain-of-title review — not just looking at who is named on the face of the patent — is essential before any transaction.

What to Look For in the Assignment Record

  • Gaps in the chain: An assignment from Inventor A to Company X, followed by a merger where the surviving entity is Company Y, requires a properly documented conveyance to Company Y. A missing link means Company Y may not hold clean title.
  • Partial assignments: Some inventors assign rights only for specific fields of use or geographies. A patent can have different owners in different markets.
  • Security interests: A lender may have a recorded security interest in a patent portfolio. If the borrower defaults, that interest can affect who controls the patents.
  • Inventor assignment agreements: These should be reviewed, not just assumed. A boilerplate employment agreement that predates the invention may not cover it, particularly if the invention was made outside normal working hours or outside the scope of the employee's role.

How This Plays Out in Transactions

In an acquisition, the buyer is purchasing the assignee's rights — which are only as good as the chain of title behind them. If even one co-inventor never signed an assignment, that person retains an undivided ownership interest that did not transfer in the deal. The buyer owns a large share of nothing enforceable against a licensee who got rights from that co-inventor.

In a licensing negotiation, a licensee should confirm that the licensor actually holds the rights it claims to be licensing. A company that is the named assignee but lacks a signed assignment from one inventor cannot grant a complete license.

In an investment context, a patent portfolio listed as a company asset should be verified against USPTO assignment records. Valuing patents the company does not cleanly own overstates the asset base.

Practical Takeaways

  • The inventor and the assignee are legally distinct — never assume the company named in a deal owns all the rights simply because its name appears on the patent.
  • Pull the full assignment history from the USPTO for every patent in a portfolio before closing any transaction.
  • Verify that every listed inventor signed a written assignment to the company and that the agreement actually covers the invention in question.
  • Look for recorded security interests, which can encumber patent rights even when title appears clean.
  • Inventorship errors can be corrected in many cases, but they require formal USPTO proceedings — budget time and cost for this if issues surface during review.
  • When the assignment record and the corporate history do not align, treat the gap as a defect that requires a legal opinion before the deal proceeds.
