When Your Startup Pivots: What Happens to Patents Filed for the Old Product

A startup pivot does not automatically transfer, void, or redirect existing patents, and founders who ignore that gap can lose protection for the new product or create disputes with investors and employees down the road

2026-11-14 · startup · patent ownership · pivot · continuation applications

Startups pivot. A team that filed a patent on a subscription analytics dashboard last year may be building an AI-driven logistics tool today. The patent application is still sitting in the USPTO queue — assigned, perhaps licensed, definitely tied to a legal entity — and the new product may share almost nothing with the claims that were drafted. What happens to that application, and what do you need to think through before you assume it still protects you?

The Patent Follows the Claims, Not the Business Direction

A patent or pending application covers exactly what the claims say it covers. When your company pivots, the claims do not automatically expand to follow the new product. If your original claims recite a method of analyzing user-subscription churn using a particular data model, those claims protect that method — not whatever your team is building now.

This creates two distinct problems:

  • Coverage gap. The new product may not fall within any existing claim. If a competitor copies the new product, the old patent may give you no basis to act.
  • Wasted prosecution costs. You may continue paying attorney fees and USPTO fees to prosecute an application that, even if granted, protects something you no longer sell.

Neither problem resolves itself. Both require a deliberate decision.

Continuation Applications as a Bridge

If your pivot is a variation on the original invention — the core technical idea survived even if the market application changed — a continuation or continuation-in-part application may let you draft new claims aimed at what you are actually building now, while keeping the original filing date as your priority date. The window for this is not unlimited; it closes when the parent application issues as a patent or is abandoned. If a pivot is in progress and the parent is still pending, that timing question is worth raising with counsel promptly.

Ownership Does Not Automatically Follow the New Team

Pivots often come with personnel changes. Employees who invented the original product may have left. New engineers who are building the pivot may never have signed invention assignment agreements tied to the existing application. Advisors who contributed to early technical decisions may have informal equity arrangements that were never clarified.

This creates an ownership chain problem:

  • Departing inventors. An inventor who leaves does not take their inventorship credit with them, but if assignment paperwork was never completed, they may retain ownership rights in the underlying patent. Courts have found that unsigned or ambiguous assignment documents can leave title clouded.
  • New contributors. If the pivot involves genuinely new technical improvements, the engineers building those improvements are potentially inventors on any new application. If they have not signed current assignment agreements with the company, the company may not own what they create.
  • Investor documents. Seed and Series A term sheets sometimes include representations that the company owns all IP relevant to its business. A patent filed on the old product, still pending, may technically fall within that representation — even if the business has moved on.

What to Audit Before the Next Funding Round

Before a financing, acquirer, or strategic partner reviews your cap table and IP schedule, you want to know: who are the named inventors on every pending or granted application, have all of them executed valid assignment agreements to the company, and do any of those agreements contain field-of-use or reversion language that was written for the old product category?

Abandonment Is a Decision, Not a Default

Some founders simply stop paying maintenance fees or responding to office actions after a pivot, letting applications go abandoned without a formal decision. Abandonment by neglect has consequences:

  • Prior art creation. Once an application publishes (typically 18 months after filing), the published specification becomes prior art. If you later want to file on a related improvement, your own published application can be used against you.
  • Competitor opportunity. A competitor who monitors your published application now knows your technical disclosure without you having a granted patent to enforce.
  • No reinstatement guarantee. Reviving an abandoned application is possible in some circumstances but is not automatic, and unintentional abandonment has a narrow definition at the USPTO.

If the old patent is genuinely irrelevant to the new business, a deliberate decision to abandon — made with an understanding of what that publishes — is cleaner than drift.

Practical Takeaways

  • Map your existing claims against your current product before assuming a pending application protects the new direction.
  • Check the status of every pending application after a pivot — office action deadlines do not pause because your business model changed.
  • Audit inventor assignments for both departing employees and anyone new who contributed to the pivot's technical architecture.
  • Evaluate continuation timing if the core technical idea survived the pivot; the window closes at grant or abandonment of the parent.
  • Make abandonment a formal decision, not a default — understand what your specification discloses before you let a publication become prior art against you.
  • Update IP representations in financing documents to reflect the actual portfolio, not the portfolio that existed at the last round.
