Patent Reexamination: What Can Trigger It and What Happens to Your Patent

Patent reexamination is a USPTO procedure that lets anyone challenge a granted patent's validity based on prior art, and understanding how it works matters whether you hold a patent or face one

2026-11-09 · reexamination · patent validity · ex parte reexamination · post-grant proceedings

A granted patent is not permanently settled law. The USPTO has the authority to reopen examination of a patent after it issues, and that process — called reexamination — can narrow, cancel, or confirm the patent's claims. Whether you own a patent someone is challenging or you're trying to knock out a patent that's blocking you, knowing how reexamination works is practically useful.

What Reexamination Is and How It Differs From IPR

Reexamination and Inter Partes Review (IPR) are both post-grant USPTO procedures, but they are different tracks. IPR is a trial-like proceeding before the Patent Trial and Appeal Board and is the more commonly used challenge mechanism today. Reexamination is an examination-based process handled by USPTO examiners — closer in character to the original prosecution of the application.

There are two types of reexamination still in active use:

  • Ex parte reexamination: Any person — including the patent owner, a competitor, or an anonymous third party — can request this. Once the request is filed and granted, the proceeding is largely between the patent owner and the examiner. The requester steps out after the initial submission.
  • Inter partes reexamination: This track was largely replaced by IPR when the America Invents Act took effect in 2012. It is no longer available for new petitions, but legacy proceedings from before that date still exist in the record.

Ex parte reexamination remains live and is used regularly, often when a requester prefers to stay anonymous or when the prior art situation is straightforward enough that a full IPR trial feels like overkill.

What Triggers a Reexamination Request

To get reexamination started, a requester must present a substantial new question of patentability — meaning prior art patents or printed publications that raise a real question about whether the claims should have been granted. This is a lower bar than proving invalidity outright, but it is not trivial. The USPTO will refuse a request that simply recycles art the examiner already considered.

The prior art must consist of patents or printed publications. Reexamination does not allow challenges based on prior public use, prior sales, or prior invention by another person — those grounds are better suited to IPR or district court litigation. If your challenge depends on something other than documentary prior art, reexamination is probably not your vehicle.

Anyone can file. A competitor can request reexamination of your patent without ever identifying themselves, because the rules permit anonymous requests submitted through a registered attorney or agent.

What Happens During Reexamination

If the USPTO grants the request — which it does in the substantial majority of cases where a proper substantial new question is presented — examination reopens. The patent owner receives office actions and has the opportunity to respond, amend claims, and argue against the prior art, just as during original prosecution.

Claim Amendments During Reexamination

The patent owner can amend claims to distinguish the newly cited prior art, but there are constraints. Claims cannot be broadened during reexamination. Any amendment narrows the scope, and the same prosecution history estoppel principles that apply during original prosecution apply here — arguments made to get through reexamination can limit how the claims are interpreted later in litigation.

The Outcome

At the end of reexamination, the USPTO issues a reexamination certificate. That certificate either confirms the original claims, cancels some or all claims, or incorporates amended claims. The certificate becomes part of the patent's permanent record and is publicly available. Courts and future examiners can see exactly what survived and what did not.

If the patent owner disagrees with the examiner's final rejection, they can appeal within the USPTO to the Patent Trial and Appeal Board, and from there to federal court if necessary.

Why This Matters for Patent Holders and Challengers

For patent holders, a reexamination request — even one that ultimately confirms all claims — is disruptive and expensive. It reopens prosecution, creates new file history that defendants can use in litigation, and forces the patent owner to engage with art they may never have addressed before.

For challengers, ex parte reexamination is cheaper than IPR and can be filed anonymously. The tradeoff is that the challenger has limited ability to participate once the proceeding begins, and there is no estoppel effect — unlike IPR, losing a reexamination does not bar you from raising the same prior art in district court.

Practical Takeaways

  • Ex parte reexamination is still active and allows anonymous third-party challenges based on patents and printed publications.
  • The threshold to trigger reexamination is a substantial new question of patentability — lower than proving invalidity, but the prior art must be genuinely new to the record.
  • Patent owners can amend claims during reexamination but cannot broaden them, and every argument made becomes permanent prosecution history.
  • The reexamination certificate is public and follows the patent permanently — courts, licensees, and acquirers will see what happened.
  • Reexamination does not carry the same estoppel consequences as IPR, which affects how challengers should think about sequencing their strategy.
  • If your invalidity argument rests on prior use or prior sales rather than documentary prior art, reexamination is not the right tool.
