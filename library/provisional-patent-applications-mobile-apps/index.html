Provisional Patent Applications for Mobile Apps: What You Buy and What You Don't

A provisional patent application gives a mobile app founder a priority date and twelve months to refine an invention, but it never becomes a patent on its own and comes with real traps if you misunderstand what it covers.

2026-11-11 · provisional patent · mobile apps · patent pending · priority date

A provisional patent application is one of the most misunderstood tools in a founder's IP toolkit. It is not a patent, it does not get examined, and it will never issue as a granted right unless you follow it with a nonprovisional application within twelve months. What it does do — if prepared carefully — is lock in a priority date that can defeat prior art that emerges after you file.

What a Provisional Actually Does

When you file a provisional, the USPTO assigns it a filing date and stores it. That date becomes your earliest possible priority date for the subject matter disclosed. If a competitor publishes something similar six months after your provisional, your earlier date can be used to show that publication is not prior art against your later nonprovisional.

The twelve-month window that opens on your provisional filing date is sometimes called the "patent pending" period. You can legitimately use that phrase on your app, marketing materials, and investor decks once a provisional is on file. That phrase carries real commercial weight — it signals to competitors that a filing exists — but it is a description of status, not a guarantee of protection.

Provisionals are also useful for founders who need to show an invention at a conference, demo day, or to a potential acquirer before they are ready to file a full nonprovisional. The provisional buys time without permanently committing to claim language.

What a Provisional Does Not Do

This is where founders consistently get into trouble.

It does not protect what you do not disclose

A provisional has no claims. Claims are what define the legal boundary of a patent. But the disclosure in your provisional determines what your nonprovisional claims can reach back to. If your provisional describes version one of your app and you spend eleven months building a substantially different version two, the new features have no priority date. Your nonprovisional can claim those new features, but only as of the nonprovisional filing date — not the provisional date.

This matters enormously if you have been pitching publicly, posting on social media, or shipping updates during those twelve months. Public disclosures of features not in your provisional can become prior art against your own later claims.

It does not extend your total patent term

A granted utility patent in the US lasts twenty years from the nonprovisional filing date, not from the provisional. The twelve months you spend in provisional status are not added back. If timeline to exclusivity matters to your business model, that tradeoff is worth understanding before you decide how long to ride the provisional window.

It expires automatically

If you do not file a nonprovisional — or a PCT international application claiming the provisional's priority — before the twelve-month deadline, the provisional goes abandoned. There is no extension available. The USPTO will not send you a warning. The deadline is hard.

What a Good Provisional Should Include

Because the provisional's disclosure sets the ceiling on what your nonprovisional can claim with that priority date, the quality of the provisional document matters.

  • Full written description of every feature you intend to claim. Courts and examiners have consistently held that you cannot claim priority to subject matter that was not adequately disclosed in the provisional.
  • Drawings or screenshots where the app's functionality is visual. For mobile apps, the user interface flow often encodes core inventive steps. Diagrams of screen states, data flows, and decision logic are worth including.
  • System and method descriptions. Mobile app inventions often have both a system dimension (the arrangement of components) and a method dimension (the steps a user or server performs). Describing both in the provisional preserves your options.
  • Edge cases and variations. Competitors and examiners look for ways to design around narrow disclosures. Describing alternative implementations in your provisional gives your later claims more room.

A bare-bones provisional — a slide deck or pitch summary filed to get a date — often creates a false sense of security. The date is real, but the subject matter it covers may be far narrower than the founder assumes.

Practical Takeaways

  • A provisional gives you a priority date and twelve months, not patent protection — treat those as separate things.
  • The disclosure in your provisional sets the ceiling for what your nonprovisional can claim with that date; thin provisionals produce thin priority.
  • Features added to your app after the provisional filing date have no priority from that filing.
  • Public disclosures of features not in your provisional can become prior art against your own later claims.
  • The twelve-month deadline is a hard cutoff with no USPTO extensions — calendar it immediately after filing.
  • "Patent pending" is a legitimate and commercially meaningful phrase once a provisional is on file, but it describes a status, not an enforceable right.
