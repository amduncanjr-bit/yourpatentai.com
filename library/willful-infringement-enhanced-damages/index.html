Willful Infringement: What It Means and Why It Can Double or Triple What You Owe

Willful infringement is a finding that a defendant knew about a patent and infringed it anyway, and it can multiply a damages award up to three times under federal patent law

2026-11-06 · patent litigation · willful infringement · enhanced damages · patent damages

Patent damages are already serious — they can reach into the millions even in an average case. But courts have the power to increase that number significantly when a defendant is found to have willfully infringed. Understanding what willful infringement means, how it gets proven, and what it actually costs is essential for any company that operates near someone else's patents.

What Willful Infringement Actually Means

Willful infringement is not simply infringement that happened to be intentional. Almost all infringement is intentional in the sense that you meant to build your product. Willfulness in the patent law sense means something more specific: the defendant knew about the patent and chose to infringe it anyway, without a reasonable basis to believe that its conduct was lawful.

The standard that federal courts apply asks whether the infringer's behavior was egregious — reckless disregard for a known patent right. Knowing about a patent and doing nothing to investigate whether you infringe it, or continuing to infringe after receiving a demand letter and ignoring it, are the kinds of facts that move a case toward a willfulness finding.

Importantly, willfulness is a separate question from infringement itself. A jury can find that you infringed and that the infringement was willful, or it can find infringement without willfulness. The willfulness finding then goes to the judge, who decides whether to increase damages.

How Enhanced Damages Work

Under 35 U.S.C. § 284, a court may increase damages up to three times the amount found or assessed. This is called enhanced damages, and willfulness is the primary basis for granting them. The statute gives the judge discretion — it does not require enhancement even if willfulness is found, and courts weigh the degree of the defendant's culpability before deciding how much to increase the award.

In practice, courts look at a cluster of factors when deciding whether and how much to enhance:

  • Whether the infringer copied the patented technology directly, rather than developing independently
  • Whether the infringer had a good-faith basis for believing it did not infringe, such as a credible opinion of counsel
  • How the infringer behaved during litigation — stonewalling, destroying evidence, or asserting weak defenses in bad faith can make things worse
  • The infringer's financial size relative to the patentee — courts sometimes show more restraint when both parties are large commercial entities
  • Whether the infringer stopped or remediated once it learned of the patent

Enhanced damages are distinct from attorney's fees, though both can follow a willfulness finding. A case deemed exceptional can also trigger a fee-shifting award under § 285.

Why Demand Letters and Opinions of Counsel Matter So Much

The moment a company receives a patent demand letter, the willfulness clock starts. Before that letter, it is difficult for a patent owner to prove you knew about the patent. After it, that knowledge is documented.

This is why experienced patent counsel almost always recommend getting a written freedom-to-operate or non-infringement opinion when you receive a demand letter about a patent that covers something material to your business. An opinion of counsel does two things: it forces a real legal analysis of your exposure, and it creates a contemporaneous record that you took the patent seriously and had a reasonable basis to continue your activity.

An opinion of counsel is not a shield from infringement liability. If you infringe, you infringe. But a credible written opinion, obtained in good faith before or shortly after you learned of the patent, is strong evidence against willfulness and therefore against enhanced damages.

What Not to Do

  • Do not ignore a demand letter. Ignoring it does not make it go away; it makes the willfulness case easier to prove.
  • Do not instruct engineers to avoid reading competitor patents as a blanket policy — courts have treated this strategy skeptically and it does not insulate you from willfulness the way some people assume.
  • Do not rely on a one-paragraph email from in-house counsel as your opinion of counsel. Courts look for detailed, reasoned analysis.

Practical Takeaways

  • Willful infringement requires knowing about a patent and infringing it anyway without a reasonable legal basis — it goes beyond ordinary infringement
  • Enhanced damages under § 284 can multiply a jury's damages award up to three times, at the judge's discretion
  • A credible written opinion of counsel is the most effective tool for rebutting a willfulness allegation after you learn of a patent
  • Receiving a demand letter is the most common trigger — treat it as a legal event requiring prompt, documented response
  • How you behave during litigation matters; courts can increase damages based on litigation misconduct even beyond the underlying infringement
  • Willfulness findings also open the door to attorney's fees awards under the exceptional case doctrine, compounding the financial exposure significantly
